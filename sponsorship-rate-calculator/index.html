Sponsorship Rate Calculator

A sponsorship rate is usually built from views, not followers: take your average views per post, multiply by the CPM you want to charge, divide by 1,000, then add for extra deliverables, paid usage rights and exclusivity. The calculator above does that with your own numbers and returns a low-to-high quote range.

CPM pricing explained

CPM means cost per mille: the price for 1,000 views. It is the unit brands already buy media in, which is why quoting in CPM makes your number easy for a marketing team to compare against everything else in their budget.

The arithmetic is simple: views ÷ 1,000 × CPM. At 50,000 average views and a $20 CPM that is $1,000 for one post; at $40 it is $2,000. Quote a range rather than a point, because the brand expects to negotiate inside one.

Usage rights and exclusivity

The post itself is rarely the whole deal. Two clauses do most of the damage to an underpriced quote: usage rights and exclusivity.

Usage rights let the brand run your content beyond your own feed: as a paid ad, on their website, in email, or in a store. That is media they would otherwise pay to produce and license, so it is priced separately from the post. The calculator adds it as a percentage uplift; set the term (30 days, 90 days, in perpetuity) in the contract, because "perpetual, all channels" is a much larger ask than a 30-day paid social test.

Exclusivity stops you working with competitors for a period. It has a real cost: it removes deals you would otherwise take. Price it by category width and duration, and refuse open-ended versions. In the calculator the two uplifts stack multiplicatively, so a 20% usage uplift and a 50% exclusivity uplift produce 1.2 × 1.5 = 1.8 times the base.

Negotiating

Send a range and an itemised breakdown, not a single number. Listing the post fee, usage uplift and exclusivity uplift separately turns the conversation from "that is too expensive" into "can we drop the exclusivity", which is a negotiation you can win.

Four things worth holding to. Quote before the brand names a budget if you can, and ask for their range if you cannot. Put the usage term in months and in writing. Keep payment terms explicit (net 30 is common to ask for) along with what happens if the campaign is cancelled after you have filmed. And treat the first offer as a starting point, because it usually is.

Whatever you agree, the disclosure is not negotiable: the FTC disclosure guidance for social media influencers requires a clear and conspicuous disclosure of a material connection to the brand. Build it into the content rather than burying it in a description.

Frequently asked questions

What are the expected UGC rates in 2026?

We do not publish an expected rate, because no dated public survey supports one and the figures circulating on rate-calculator sites are recycled from each other. What you can do is build your own number: decide a base price per video that covers your filming time, then add for hook variations, raw footage and usage months. The UGC rate calculator totals that for you.

How much should I get paid for UGC?

Enough to cover your time, your equipment and the rights the brand is buying, which is a number only you can set. Work backwards: hours per video (scripting, filming, editing, revisions) at a rate you would accept, then add separately for extra hooks, raw files and any paid usage. Ship that as an itemised quote rather than one figure.

How much does an UGC cost?

It depends entirely on scope, and anyone quoting a single industry figure is guessing. The cost drivers are consistent, though: number of videos, number of hook or opening variations, whether raw footage transfers, how many revision rounds are included, and how long the brand may run the content as paid media. Price each of those as its own line so the brand can trade scope against budget.

How to charge for UGC content?

Charge per package, itemised. Start with a base price per finished video, add a per-hook price for extra openings, add a flat fee if raw footage is included, then add a monthly usage fee for paid ad rights expressed as a percentage of the base. That structure makes every change the brand requests visibly cost something, which is the point.

How many views do you need on YouTube to make $5000 a month?

That depends on the CPM you charge, so run it backwards: monthly target ÷ CPM × 1,000. At a $20 CPM assumption, $5,000 needs 250,000 sponsored views a month, which could be one video at 250,000 views or four at 62,500. At a $40 CPM it halves to 125,000. Substitute your own CPM; these are illustrations of the arithmetic, not market rates.

How much do sponsors pay for 100K views?

There is no published answer, because the price is whatever CPM you and the brand agree. The arithmetic is 100,000 ÷ 1,000 × CPM: $2,000 at a $20 CPM assumption, $4,000 at $40. Usage rights and exclusivity are added on top of that base. Enter your own CPM range in the calculator above rather than trusting a headline figure from elsewhere.

Do YouTubers get paid if you skip sponsors?

Yes, in most deals. Sponsor segments are normally bought in advance as a flat fee, or priced against the video's views, so the payment does not depend on whether individual viewers watch the segment. The exception is performance-based deals built on affiliate links or discount codes, where earnings track conversions instead. Many contracts combine a flat fee with a performance component.

How many YouTube views do I need to make $10,000 a month?

Divide the target by your CPM and multiply by 1,000. At a $20 CPM assumption, $10,000 a month needs 500,000 sponsored views; at $40 it needs 250,000. Those figures assume every video carries a sold integration, which is rarely true, so plan for unsold months. Use your own CPM and your median view count in the calculator rather than these illustrative numbers.
